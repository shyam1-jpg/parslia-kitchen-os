Jakarta Travel Guide

Jakarta is Indonesia's sprawling capital and Southeast Asia's largest metropolitan area, a place of gleaming malls, traffic-filled toll roads and leafy colonial-era neighbourhoods. It is rarely anyone's dream destination at first glance, yet it rewards curiosity: the Dutch-built old town of Kota Tua, vibrant markets, one of the largest mosques in Asia facing a neo-Gothic cathedral, and some of the best food in the archipelago. It is also the gateway to the Thousand Islands, Bandung's highlands and onward flights across Indonesia's many islands.

When to Go

Jakarta is hot and humid all year. The dry season (roughly May–September) is the best time to visit, with fewer heavy downpours. The wet season (roughly November–March) brings intense rain, and January and February can see flooding in parts of the city that disrupts traffic. During Ramadan, many restaurants are quieter by day and lively after sunset, and the Eid al-Fitr holiday (Lebaran) sees a mass exodus from the city, making the capital unusually calm but transport out of it very busy.

Top Attractions

  1. Kota Tua (Old Town) – Fatahillah Square, the Jakarta History Museum in the old city hall, the Wayang Puppet Museum and colonial buildings; rent a colourful bicycle to ride around the square.
  2. National Monument (Monas) – The 132-metre obelisk at the centre of Merdeka Square, with an observation deck and a history museum in its base.
  3. Istiqlal Mosque and Jakarta Cathedral – Southeast Asia's largest mosque stands opposite the cathedral, a symbol of religious tolerance; visitors can join guided tours outside prayer times.
  4. National Museum of Indonesia – Archaeological and ethnographic collections from across the archipelago.
  5. Taman Mini Indonesia Indah – A park with pavilions representing Indonesia's provinces and traditional architecture.
  6. Sunda Kelapa harbour – The old port where wooden phinisi schooners still load cargo.
  7. Thousand Islands (Kepulauan Seribu) – Boat trips to islands north of the city for snorkelling and beaches.
  8. Glodok (Chinatown) – Temples, herbal shops and street food in one of the city's oldest districts.

What to Eat

Indonesian food is varied and often spicy. Try nasi goreng, satay with peanut sauce, soto Betawi (a rich local beef soup), gado-gado (vegetables with peanut sauce, a good vegetarian choice), kerak telor (a Betawi egg-and-rice snack sold around Monas and Kota Tua), rendang at Padang restaurants and martabak sweet or savoury pancakes at night. Street stalls and food courts in malls are inexpensive, and Menteng and Senopati have stylish cafés. Indian restaurants are found in central and southern business areas.

Getting There and Around

Soekarno–Hatta International Airport (CGK) is Jakarta's main airport, connected by the airport train to the city centre, by DAMRI buses and taxis. Halim Perdanakusuma (HLP) serves some domestic flights. The Whoosh high-speed train links Jakarta with Bandung.

Traffic is notorious, so plan around rush hours. The TransJakarta bus rapid transit system, the MRT north–south line, the LRT and KRL commuter trains are the fastest ways to cross the city. Use a contactless card such as those issued by Indonesian banks for transit. App-based rides (Grab, Gojek), including motorbike taxis, are widely used and affordable; Bluebird taxis are reliable metered cabs.

Where to Stay

  • Central Jakarta (Thamrin, Menteng): Near Monas, museums and the MRT, with international hotels.
  • South Jakarta (SCBD, Senopati, Kemang): Upscale dining, nightlife and business hotels.
  • Kota and Glodok: Close to the old town and Chinatown, good for heritage lovers.
  • Near the airport (Tangerang): Practical for transit stays.

Budget Tips

  • Eat at warungs and mall food courts for cheap, tasty meals.
  • Use TransJakarta, MRT and commuter trains rather than taxis in peak hours.
  • Many museums charge only small entrance fees, and mosques and parks are free.
  • Luxury hotels in Jakarta are often good value compared with other Asian capitals; compare carefully.
  • Travel outside the Lebaran holiday when prices and crowds spike.

Practical Information

  • Visa: Entry rules change and depend on your passport. Use the destination country's official government site and your own government's travel advice. This guide does not say who can enter.
  • Currency: Indonesian rupiah (IDR). Cards work in malls and hotels; carry cash for street food.
  • Language: Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia); English is spoken in hotels and business areas.
  • Safety: This guide does not rate safety. Read your government's current travel advice before you go.
  • Etiquette: Dress modestly, especially at religious sites, and use your right hand for giving and receiving.
