Barcelona Travel Guide

The City in Brief

Barcelona packs an unusual amount into one compact city: a medieval old town, a beach, hills with sweeping views and the extraordinary buildings of Antoni Gaudí and other Catalan Modernista architects. It is the capital of Catalonia, with its own language, festivals and fiercely proud food culture. Days here tend to start late and end even later; lunch is the main meal and dinner rarely begins before 9pm. Walking is the best way to soak it up, from the narrow lanes of the Gothic Quarter to the grid of grand boulevards in the Eixample.

When to Go

  • Spring (April–June): Warm, sunny and ideal for sightseeing. Sant Jordi (23 April), when people exchange books and roses, is a lovely day to be in town.
  • Summer (July–August): Hot and very busy, with beach days, open-air concerts and the Gràcia street festival in August. Many locals take holidays in August, and accommodation is at its priciest.
  • Autumn (September–October): Sea still warm, crowds thinning, and the La Mercè festival in late September with human towers (castells) and fireworks.
  • Winter (November–March): Mild by northern European standards, though not beach weather. Fewer queues and often lower hotel rates.

Top Attractions

  1. Sagrada Família – Gaudí's still-unfinished basilica, with forest-like columns and stained glass that floods the interior with colour. Timed tickets should be booked in advance.
  2. Park Güell – A hillside park of mosaic benches and whimsical pavilions overlooking the city; the monumental zone requires a timed ticket.
  3. Gothic Quarter (Barri Gòtic) – Barcelona Cathedral, Plaça Reial and hidden squares built over Roman foundations.
  4. Casa Batlló and Casa Milà (La Pedrera) – Two of Gaudí's most famous homes on Passeig de Gràcia, with rooftops that look like something from a fairy tale.
  5. La Rambla and La Boqueria – The famous boulevard is busy and touristy, but the Boqueria food market just off it is worth visiting early in the day.
  6. El Born and the Picasso Museum – Elegant lanes, the Gothic Santa Maria del Mar church and the museum devoted to Picasso's early work.
  7. Montjuïc – A hill with gardens, the castle, the Joan Miró Foundation and the National Art Museum of Catalonia (MNAC), reached by cable car or funicular.
  8. Barceloneta and the beaches – City beaches lined with seafood restaurants and beach bars; Bogatell and Nova Icària are usually less crowded.

Food to Try

Eat like a local by sharing tapas: patatas bravas, pan con tomate (bread rubbed with tomato and olive oil), jamón ibérico, croquetas and pimientos de padrón. Seafood paella and its noodle cousin fideuà are best at lunchtime near the sea. Try Catalan dishes such as escalivada (roasted vegetables), botifarra sausage and crema catalana for dessert. Look for good-value menú del día set lunches on weekdays. Vermouth before lunch on weekends is a local ritual, and the Raval district has many South Asian restaurants.

Getting There and Getting Around

Barcelona–El Prat Airport (BCN) is linked to the city by the Aerobús, the metro line L9 Sud and suburban trains. Some budget airlines use Girona or Reus airports, both much further away. High-speed trains connect Barcelona Sants with Madrid, Valencia and France.

The metro is efficient and covers most attractions, supported by buses and trams. Multi-trip T-casual tickets or a Hola Barcelona travel card are good value for visitors. The centre is very walkable, and cycling along the seafront is popular.

Where to Stay

  • Eixample: Elegant, central and well connected, close to Gaudí's buildings; plenty of mid-range and upscale hotels.
  • Gothic Quarter and El Born: Atmospheric and walkable, but can be noisy at night.
  • Gràcia: Village-like squares, independent shops and a local feel.
  • Barceloneta and Poblenou: Near the beach; Poblenou is quieter and more residential.
  • Sants and Poble-sec: Often better value, with good transport links and lively tapas streets.

Budget Tips

  • Buy attraction tickets online; some sites are cheaper or only available with timed entry.
  • Many museums are free on certain Sunday afternoons or on the first Sunday of the month; check each one.
  • Eat the weekday menú del día rather than à la carte dinners.
  • Avoid restaurants on La Rambla, where prices tend to be higher.
  • Free experiences include the beaches, the Bunkers del Carmel viewpoint, and wandering the Gothic Quarter.
  • Hotels are often cheaper midweek and outside major trade fairs.
